\begin{abstract}
Legislative vote-outcome prediction in multiparty systems is challenging due to complex coalition dynamics and temporal dependencies. Unlike most prior work that focuses on predicting individual roll-call votes, we address proposition-level outcome prediction, i.e., whether a proposition is approved or rejected in the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies. We propose VOTE-RAP, a feature-based approach that uses leakage-safe temporal and structural features derived from legislative history. We construct a dataset of 8,914 voting sessions from 2003 to 2024 and train an XGBoost classifier evaluated with a chronological 80/20 train--test split. In a reproducible comparison against simple baselines and a state-of-the-art roll-call-based method evaluated in a generalization setting, VOTE-RAP achieves AUROC 0.908 and rejected-class F1 0.703, substantially outperforming both the government-orientation baseline (AUROC 0.739, F1 0.500) and the roll-call-based model (AUROC 0.500, F1 0.000, equivalent to the majority-class baseline). Beyond predictive performance, these results support practical use in legislative monitoring by enabling earlier, leakage-safe signals of approval or rejection that can help prioritize analysis and oversight, strengthening information transparency and decision-making for stakeholders such as oversight institutions, journalists, and civil-society organizations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{introduction}

Predicting legislative vote outcomes in multiparty systems is a challenging task due to frequent coalition changes, institutional heterogeneity, and strong temporal dependencies. In the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies, where 513 deputies from multiple parties interact under shifting alliances, whether a proposition is approved or rejected depends not only on ideological alignment but also on institutional signals and recent legislative dynamics. With the growing availability of open legislative data, including roll-call votes, proposition metadata, and deputy and party information, computational methods have become an important tool for modeling and predicting legislative outcomes. Accurate and transparent outcome forecasts can have practical value for legislative monitoring and oversight, helping analysts and institutions anticipate likely approvals or rejections and prioritize scrutiny of proposals and voting sessions under time and resource constraints.

Current computational literature effectively addresses specific sub-problems but often does not explicitly address the operational goal of outcome prediction. Political science models traditionally prioritize interpretability, using ideal-point estimation to map the ideological structure of the house \citep{Poole1985,Clinton2004}. In parallel, machine learning approaches typically focus on the micro-task of predicting individual roll-call votes using text, embeddings, or meta-features \citep{Kraft2021,Feng2022PAR,Patil2020,Viola2022DataCentricBrazil,Albuquerque2025Forecasting}, whereas network science methods detect community structures within voting graphs \citep{Ferreira2021,Cherepnalkoski2022}. Despite their effectiveness in predicting individual legislators' voting decisions, these deputy-centric models do not directly address the macro challenge of aggregating individual voting behavior into a single binary outcome under institutional momentum and temporal dependencies.

Among the smaller set of studies that explicitly target proposition-level outcomes or legislative process success \citep{Bari2021AIBillPassageUS}, important methodological limitations remain. While these works predict approval at the level of individual propositions, they often rely on procedural metadata or representations constructed using future information, introducing temporal leakage that limits their applicability to real-time forecasting. Moreover, retrospective evaluation protocols frequently fail to account for evolving political dynamics, masking the difficulty of predicting outcomes in a live legislative environment. Consequently, there remains a lack of leakage-safe, feature-based systems capable of capturing short-term approval patterns at voting time.

This work proposes VOTE-RAP (Vote Outcome Prediction using Temporal Evidence of Related Approval Patterns), a system that relies exclusively on information available prior to the voting session. By engineering interpretable temporal features, specifically Government Orientation, rolling Party Popularity, and Historical Approval Rate (HAR), we capture the institutional and coalition dynamics that drive outcomes without sacrificing auditability.

\par
From an applied perspective, we frame VOTE-RAP as an \emph{early-warning / prioritization tool} for legislative monitoring. In settings where analysts (e.g., oversight bodies, journalists, civil-society organizations, advisory teams) face a high volume of propositions and limited attention, leakage-safe outcome probabilities can help prioritize which upcoming votes merit deeper scrutiny, while preserving transparency through an explicitly defined feature set.


We validate this approach through a rigorous chronological evaluation, distinguishing our work from studies that utilize random train--test splits which destroy temporal order. To quantify the generalization limitations of deputy-centric methods, we benchmark VOTE-RAP against institutional baselines and a state-of-the-art high-dimensional pipeline adapted from \citet{Albuquerque2025Forecasting}. While Albuquerque et al. focus on individual legislator prediction using 219 features and achieve approximately 85\% accuracy within their roll-call domain, their approach requires roll-call vote data available for only 9\% of propositions. We test whether such methods can generalize to the broader proposition space.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item Leakage-Safe Feature Engineering Protocol: We introduce a compact set of temporal and structural features, including leakage-safe Party Popularity and HAR, designed to capture legislative momentum without look-ahead bias.
\item Proposition-Level Prediction Model: Unlike deputy-centric models \citep{Kraft2021,Patil2020,Albuquerque2025Forecasting}, VOTE-RAP targets the final legislative outcome, effectively handling the class imbalance between approved and rejected propositions.
\item Reproducible Chronological Benchmark: We evaluate our model on a dataset of 8,914 voting sessions spanning 21 years (2003--2024) using a strict chronological 80/20 split. We demonstrate that VOTE-RAP (AUROC 0.908) significantly outperforms both the government-orientation baseline (AUROC 0.739) and the Albuquerque-style high-dimensional model tested in a generalization setting (AUROC 0.500), revealing that deputy-centric approaches fail to transfer to the broader proposition space.
\item Novelty under Leakage-Safe Generalization: To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate, under a strict leakage-safe, proposition-level generalization setting, that roll-call-dependent models fail when applied beyond their native data regime.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{related_work}

Research on legislative behavior modeling and prediction spans political science, natural language processing, and network science. We organize the literature into three main directions: (i) individual deputy vote prediction, (ii) network-based coalition analysis, and (iii) proposition-level outcome prediction.

\subsection{Individual Deputy Vote Prediction}

The largest body of work focuses on predicting how individual legislators vote on roll-call items. In political science, latent ideology and ideal-point models estimate legislator positions on ideological dimensions from voting patterns \citep{Poole1985,Clinton2004}. These frameworks are highly interpretable and support substantive analysis of legislative behavior, but they are primarily designed for structural inference rather than predictive forecasting.

In NLP and machine learning, text-based approaches leverage bill content to predict roll-call behavior. \citet{Gerrish2011} demonstrated that bill text features can be predictive, and subsequent work advanced this line with embedding-based and neural architectures \citep{Kraft2021,Patil2020}. Recent representation learning approaches incorporate political-actor and social-context information to build richer embeddings \citep{Feng2022PAR}. These methods achieve strong predictive performance but increase modeling complexity and reduce auditability, limiting their usefulness when transparent explanations are required. Related work also incorporates heterogeneous features such as legislator and party attributes and external knowledge to improve generalization \citep{Kornilova2018,Cheng2017,MultiFactor2019}.


High-dimensional feature engineering represents a parallel approach. \citet{Albuquerque2025Forecasting} propose a machine learning pipeline using 219 features, including deputy characteristics, party attributes, voting history, and proposition metadata, to predict individual deputy votes in the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies. Using ensemble methods (Random Forests, Gradient Boosting), they achieve approximately 85\% accuracy at the individual vote level. However, their approach requires roll-call vote data, which is only available for a small subset of propositions (approximately 9\% in our dataset), limiting its applicability to the broader legislative monitoring task.

Despite their effectiveness in predicting individual legislators' voting decisions, these deputy-centric models do not directly address the macro challenge of predicting proposition-level outcomes under institutional momentum and temporal dependencies.

\subsection{Network-Based Coalition Analysis}

A complementary trajectory uses complex networks to study coalitions, polarization, and voting structure. These works build vote similarity or signed networks to identify communities and alliance dynamics \citep{Brito2020,Ferreira2021,Cherepnalkoski2022,Arinik2020,Silva2025SBSI}. Network-based perspectives provide valuable descriptive insight into coalition formation and bloc behavior. However, translating network structure into proposition-level outcome predictions requires additional modeling steps, and results can be sensitive to graph construction choices and temporal instability in coalitions.


\subsection{Proposition-Level Outcome Prediction}

The smallest body of work directly targets proposition-level outcomes, which is the task addressed by VOTE-RAP. \citet{Bari2021AIBillPassageUS} predict bill-level success in the United States Congress using supervised ML with engineered institutional and metadata features. Their approach demonstrates the feasibility of predicting legislative outcomes at the proposition level, but it leverages procedural and workflow-related metadata that is tailored to the U.S. Congress; in contrast, we focus on leakage-safe temporal and structural signals that are available in the Brazilian Chamber’s open-data setting.

Proposition-level prediction faces distinct challenges compared to individual vote prediction: (i) severe class imbalance, as most propositions are approved; (ii) limited availability of roll-call data for many propositions; and (iii) the need for features that generalize across different proposition types. Moreover, when temporal ordering is not enforced, for example, through random train--test splits or feature representations built using statistics over the full dataset, evaluation can inadvertently incorporate future information, which limits applicability to real-time forecasting in dynamic legislatures \citep{Patil2020,Kraft2021,Viola2022DataCentricBrazil}. Retrospective evaluation protocols that ignore chronological structure also fail to account for evolving political dynamics.

VOTE-RAP addresses these gaps by engineering leakage-safe temporal features that capture legislative momentum without look-ahead bias, and by using a strict chronological evaluation protocol that reflects real-world forecasting conditions.

\section{Data Collection and Preparation}
\label{data}

Our dataset is constructed from official open data released by the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies,\footnote{\url{https://dadosabertos.camara.leg.br/swagger/api.html}} which include (i) roll-call voting records providing deputy votes for each session, (ii) proposition metadata (type, authorship, and key dates), and (iii) deputy and party attributes and legislature identifiers to support party-based signals and time indexing. Identifiers are standardized across sources and entity mappings (proposition, deputy, party, legislature) are resolved to ensure consistent linkage between votes, propositions, and their political context.

To form a clean prediction dataset, only sessions associated with propositions that have an unambiguous final outcome (approved or rejected) are retained, excluding cases with missing or unclear status. To prevent temporal leakage, all engineered features for a voting session are computed strictly from information available prior to that session, and any rolling statistics (e.g., party popularity, HAR) are computed using only earlier sessions. A chronological evaluation protocol is then adopted by splitting the data into 80\% training (7,131 sessions) and 20\% testing (1,783 sessions) without shuffling. Figure~\ref{fig:data_pipeline} summarizes the dataset construction and leakage-safe feature computation workflow.

The final dataset contains 8,914 voting sessions spanning 2003 to 2024 (legislatures 52 to 57) and exhibits class imbalance, with approximately 80\% approved propositions and 20\% rejected propositions. Additional descriptive trends are reported through aggregated results in Section~\ref{results}.

\section{Feature Engineering}
\label{features}

Let each voting session be indexed by $i \in \{1,\dots,N\}$, with timestamp $t_i$, proposition identifier $p_i \in \mathcal{P}$, and binary outcome $y_i \in \{0,1\}$, where $y_i=1$ denotes approval and $y_i=0$ denotes rejection. To enforce temporal integrity, all features for session $i$ are computed using only sessions $j$ such that $t_j < t_i$. We define a mapping $g(i)$ that assigns each session to a group label representing either the author’s party (when the author is a deputy) or the institutional author type (otherwise). We also use authorship metadata available prior to voting to derive coalition-size indicators.

The final VOTE-RAP feature set consists of six leakage-safe signals: \texttt{party\_\allowbreak popularity} (recency-based group approval rate over the last five prior sessions), \texttt{gov\_\allowbreak orientation}, \texttt{historical\_\allowbreak approval\_\allowbreak rate} (HAR), \texttt{num\_\allowbreak authors\_\allowbreak trunc}, \texttt{has\_\allowbreak more\_\allowbreak than\_\allowbreak 10\_\allowbreak authors}, and \texttt{popularity} (author popularity). The proposed features are defined in detail as follows.

\subsection{Party Popularity}
\label{sec:party_popularity}
To capture short-term momentum, we define party popularity as a leakage-safe recency-based approval rate for the group label $g(i)$. Let $\mathcal{H}_i(h)$ be the set of indices of prior sessions for group label $h$:
\[
\mathcal{H}_i(h) = \{ j : t_j < t_i \ \wedge \ g(j)=h \}.
\]
We use a fixed recency window of size $K$ by taking the $K$ most recent elements of $\mathcal{H}_i(g(i))$, denoted $\mathcal{H}^{(K)}_i$. Party popularity is then computed as
\[
x^{\mathrm{pp}}_i =
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}^{(K)}_i|}\sum\limits_{j \in \mathcal{H}^{(K)}_i} y_j, & \text{if } |\mathcal{H}^{(K)}_i| > 0,\\
0, & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\]
where the default value 0 is used when no prior session exists for the corresponding group. In our experiments, we set $K=5$; the window-size selection procedure is described in Section~\ref{sec:pp_window_selection} and illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:party_popularity_window}.


\subsection{Government Orientation}
\label{sec:gov_orientation}

Government orientation encodes the executive recommendation associated with the session. The raw Chamber open-data exports provide two government-orientation fields, denoted $G^{(1)}_i$ (GOV.) and $G^{(2)}_i$ (Governo). In practice, these labels reflect the same underlying information, but they appear in different periods and are sometimes filled differently depending on how the session metadata was recorded. To unify these records into a single leakage-safe signal, we resolve them into one indicator $x^{\mathrm{gov}}_i \in \{-1,0,1\}$ using the deterministic rule
\[
x^{\mathrm{gov}}_i =
\begin{cases}
G^{(1)}_i, & \text{if } G^{(1)}_i = G^{(2)}_i,\\
G^{(1)}_i, & \text{if } G^{(1)}_i \neq 0,\\
G^{(2)}_i, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
Values $-1,0,1$ represent recommendation against, neutral or undefined, and in favor of approval, respectively. When this information is unavailable, the feature is treated as missing and handled as described in Section~\ref{modeling}.


\subsection{Historical Approval Rate (HAR)}
\label{sec:har}

Some propositions appear in multiple voting sessions. We capture proposition-specific memory via a leakage-safe historical approval rate conditioned on the proposition identifier. For a given session $i$, define the set of prior sessions that concern the same proposition, using the proposition linkage provided in the dataset (i.e., the field that associates each voted proposition with its related proposition record used to aggregate repeated votes):
\[
\mathcal{P}_i = \{ j : t_j < t_i \ \wedge \ p_j = p_i \}.
\]
The Historical Approval Rate feature is defined as
\[
x^{\mathrm{har}}_i =
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_i|}\sum\limits_{j \in \mathcal{P}_i} y_j, & \text{if } |\mathcal{P}_i| > 0,\\
0.5, & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\]
where 0.5 is a neutral prior used when the proposition has no recorded previous session. Figure~\ref{fig:har_rules_comparison} compares simple proposition-history heuristics and highlights the Historical Probability Rule, which corresponds exactly to $x^{\mathrm{har}}_i$ with the 0.5 default.

\subsection{Coalition-Size Indicators (Authorship-Based)}
\label{sec:coalition_size}

We capture coalition-size effects using authorship metadata available prior to voting. Let $n^{\mathrm{auth}}_i$ denote the number of distinct authors associated with proposition $p_i$ in session $i$. We define two indicators:
\[
x^{\mathrm{na}}_i = \min\{n^{\mathrm{auth}}_i, 10\},
\qquad
x^{\mathrm{gt10}}_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } n^{\mathrm{auth}}_i > 10,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
The truncated count $x^{\mathrm{na}}_i$ corresponds to \texttt{num\_authors\_trunc}, limiting sensitivity to very long author lists (outliers) while preserving monotonic information among typical coalition sizes. We cap at 10 to keep the feature scale interpretable and robust, and to align it with the companion indicator $x^{\mathrm{gt10}}_i$ that explicitly marks very broad sponsorship. The indicator $x^{\mathrm{gt10}}_i$ corresponds to \texttt{has\_more\_than\_10\_authors} and captures very broad sponsorship. Both features are leakage-safe because they depend only on proposition authorship metadata available before the session outcome is observed.

\subsection{Author Popularity}
\label{sec:author_popularity}

In addition to \texttt{party\_\allowbreak popularity}, we incorporate an author-level signal capturing how supportive the legislature has been of propositions associated with a given author in prior roll-call votes. Let $a_i$ denote the author identifier associated with session $i$ (deputy author when applicable, or the corresponding institutional author type otherwise). For each deputy-level vote $v$ cast in a prior session $j$, define $r_v \in \{0,1\}$ as an indicator of a ``yes'' vote (in the raw data, \texttt{voto}=\texttt{sim}), and let $t_v$ be its timestamp. We also assign each vote an importance weight $w_v \in (0,1]$ proportional to the session size, computed as the session's total number of votes normalized by the maximum total-votes value observed in the dataset. This weighting gives slightly more influence to large roll-calls, which tend to reflect broader plenary participation, while keeping the scale bounded for stability.

We compute popularity for session $i$ using only prior votes associated with the same author and occurring within a one-year window:
\[
\mathcal{V}_i(a_i) = \{ v : t_v < t_i \ \wedge \ a_v = a_i \ \wedge \ (t_i - t_v) \leq 365\text{ days} \}.
\]
We use a one-year window to balance recency with statistical reliability: it is long enough to provide a meaningful history for frequent authors, while limiting drift across distant political contexts. To emphasize recent behavior, we define a time-decayed, importance-weighted popularity with exponential half-life $H=90$ days. Let $\lambda = \ln(2)/H$. The 90-day half-life provides a simple, interpretable recency prior: votes about three months old contribute half as much as very recent votes, capturing short-term dynamics without discarding older observations abruptly. The weighted popularity is
\[
x^{\mathrm{wpop}}_i =
\begin{cases}
\frac{\sum\limits_{v \in \mathcal{V}_i(a_i)} w_v \exp\!\big(-\lambda (t_i-t_v)\big)\, r_v}
     {\sum\limits_{v \in \mathcal{V}_i(a_i)} w_v \exp\!\big(-\lambda (t_i-t_v)\big)}, & \text{if the denominator $>0$,}\\[8pt]
0.5, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
In parallel, we compute a windowed (unweighted) approval proxy with a minimum-votes smoothing threshold $M=5$:
\[
x^{\mathrm{rpop}}_i =
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_i(a_i)|}\sum\limits_{v \in \mathcal{V}_i(a_i)} r_v, & \text{if } |\mathcal{V}_i(a_i)| \ge M,\\[6pt]
\frac{\sum\limits_{v \in \mathcal{V}_i(a_i)} r_v + (M-|\mathcal{V}_i(a_i)|)\cdot 0.5}{M}, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
The smoothing threshold $M=5$ prevents extreme values for authors with very few observations; when fewer than five prior votes are available in the one-year window, we shrink the estimate toward 0.5 (a neutral prior), which reduces variance and improves robustness early in an author's observed history.

Finally, the author popularity feature used by VOTE-RAP (\texttt{popularity}) combines a stable recency-sensitive estimate with a simple frequency-based estimate:
\[
x^{\mathrm{pop}}_i = 0.7\,x^{\mathrm{wpop}}_i + 0.3\,x^{\mathrm{rpop}}_i.
\]
We weight the decayed, importance-weighted component more heavily (0.7) because it directly encodes short-term dynamics and session salience, while retaining a smaller contribution (0.3) from the raw windowed rate as a conservative anchor that reduces sensitivity to the specific decay profile. In our implementation, $x^{\mathrm{pop}}_i$ is precomputed in chronological order using only votes strictly prior to $t_i$ and merged into the vote-session dataset by session identifier; any remaining missing values after merging are handled during preprocessing as described in Section~\ref{modeling}.

\section{Modeling Approach}
\label{modeling}

This section describes the learning algorithm and the evaluation protocol used to predict proposition-level outcomes, including the temporally ordered train--test split, hyperparameter tuning on training data only, imbalance handling, and preprocessing steps.

\subsection{Algorithm Selection}
We model proposition-level outcomes with gradient-boosted decision trees using XGBoost \citep{Chen2016XGBoost}. This choice is motivated by practical considerations aligned with \emph{Expert Systems with Applications}: (i) our inputs are heterogeneous but structured (institutional signals, coalition-size indicators, and temporal rates), which tree ensembles handle naturally without heavy feature transformation; (ii) boosting captures nonlinearities and interactions (e.g., orientation signals interacting with temporal momentum) while remaining stable under mixed feature scales; and (iii) the resulting system remains auditable, since each prediction is driven by a small, explicitly defined set of variables whose global importance can be inspected.
\par
In XGBoost, an ensemble is built additively by fitting a sequence of decision trees, where each new tree is trained to reduce the remaining error (gradient) of a regularized loss objective. The model outputs an approval probability $\hat{p}_i \in [0,1]$ for each session $i$ using the logistic objective.

\subsection{Temporal Train-Test Split}
To preserve temporal realism and reflect real-world usage, we order sessions chronologically by date and split the dataset into an 80\% training prefix and a 20\% test suffix. This mirrors deployment in which the system is trained on past sessions and then used to score future votes. All model selection and preprocessing decisions are performed using only the training split, and the final model is evaluated once on the held-out test split.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization}
We tune hyperparameters via randomized search over 75 sampled configurations, using AUROC as the selection metric. In our implementation, the search uses 3-fold stratified cross-validation over the training split (with fixed random seed), and the best configuration is retrained on the full training split and evaluated once on the held-out test split. This design keeps the pipeline reproducible while limiting opportunities for implicit adaptation to the test suffix.

\subsection{Class Imbalance Handling}
Because rejected propositions are the minority class, we mitigate imbalance by up-weighting minority-class errors during training via \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}, computed from the class frequencies in the training data. Intuitively, this makes the learner treat mistakes on the minority class as more costly than mistakes on the majority class. For example, if the training split contains roughly four times more approved than rejected outcomes, setting \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} to this ratio increases the penalty of misclassifying rejected cases, encouraging the model to pay more attention to the minority class. This weighting is computed using only training labels (within each training fold and in the final training fit), without using any information from the test split.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
We remove sessions with missing outcome labels (the field \texttt{aprovacao}, i.e., the approval outcome). Missing \texttt{party\_\allowbreak popularity} values are imputed with 0, and missing \texttt{historical\_\allowbreak approval\_\allowbreak rate} values are imputed with 0.5, corresponding to a neutral prior when no proposition history exists. Since XGBoost is tree-based, feature scaling is not required, and numeric features are used in their original scale.
\par
For the reproducible comparison runs reported in Section~\ref{results}, we apply standardization to the numeric temporal features \texttt{popularity}, \texttt{party\_\allowbreak popularity}, and \texttt{historical\_\allowbreak approval\_\allowbreak rate} to keep a consistent preprocessing interface across models.


\section{Experimental Results}
\label{results}

This section reports the empirical performance of VOTE-RAP under the leakage-safe chronological evaluation protocol. We first define simple baselines and a domain-informed heuristic to contextualize performance (Section~\ref{sec:simple_baselines}), then compare VOTE-RAP against the government-orientation baseline and an Albuquerque-style high-dimensional method under identical metrics and splits (Section ~\ref{sec:comparison_albuquerque}). We further analyze minority-class (rejected) performance and assess temporal robustness through a year-by-year rolling-window evaluation.

\subsection{Party Popularity Window-Size Selection}
\label{sec:pp_window_selection}
We choose the party-popularity recency window size $K$ via model selection on temporally ordered training data only. Specifically, we compare AUROC obtained with alternative window definitions (including full-history and recency-based variants) and select the best-performing configuration for the final definition of \textit{party\_popularity}. In our experiments, $K=5$ yields the best AUROC.

\subsection{Simple Baselines}
\label{sec:simple_baselines}
To contextualize model performance, we include four simple baselines for proposition-level outcome prediction:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Random Guess}, which predicts approval or rejection uniformly at random;
    \item \emph{Majority Class}, which always predicts the majority label observed in the training split;
    \item \emph{Stratified Probability}, which predicts approval with probability equal to the training approval rate and rejection otherwise; and
    \item \emph{Government Orientation}, a domain-informed heuristic that predicts approval when \texttt{gov\_orientation}=1, rejection when \texttt{gov\_orientation}=-1, and defaults to the training majority class when \texttt{gov\_orientation}=0 or missing.
\end{enumerate}
For AUROC comparisons, we map \texttt{gov\_orientation} to a coarse probability score (0.8 for approval recommendation, 0.2 for rejection recommendation, and the training approval rate otherwise).

\subsection{Comprehensive Comparison: Baselines, VOTE-RAP, and Albuquerque Generalization}
\label{sec:comparison_albuquerque}
To enable a direct, metric-aligned comparison that tests generalization capability, we evaluate (i) the simple baselines in Section~\ref{sec:simple_baselines} and (ii) VOTE-RAP under the same chronological 80/20 split and the same evaluation metrics used throughout the paper (AUROC and rejected-class F1). We further include a roll-call-based, high-dimensional methodology as an external reference, following the approach proposed by \citet{Albuquerque2025Forecasting}.

\par
\textbf{Roll-call-based high-dimensional method (recap).}
The method of \citet{Albuquerque2025Forecasting} focuses on forecasting \emph{individual deputy votes} using a high-dimensional feature space. It constructs a large set of structured variables capturing political context, proposition characteristics, and historical voting patterns, and evaluates ensemble classifiers (e.g., Random Forest and Gradient Boosting) under a time-aware evaluation protocol.

\par
\textbf{Adaptation for proposition-level generalization.}
To test whether a roll-call-dependent pipeline can generalize to proposition-level outcome prediction, we implement a \textbf{generalization test} in which the model is trained on the roll-call subset and evaluated on the full VOTE-RAP test set. The roll-call dataset is filtered to binary votes and aggregated to the voting-session level by computing session-level means of numeric features across deputy rows; a session-level outcome label is defined by majority vote within the session. An ensemble of Random Forest and Gradient Boosting is then trained on this aggregated roll-call data.
Because the full proposition space does not provide deputy-level roll-call inputs for all propositions, legislature-level feature aggregates are computed from the roll-call data and mapped onto propositions in the VOTE-RAP test suffix via legislature identifiers, yielding a consistent feature interface for evaluation on the full test set. The trained ensemble is then evaluated on the VOTE-RAP test set using the same metrics as VOTE-RAP and the baselines.

\subsection{Overall Performance}
VOTE-RAP substantially outperforms all baselines and the Albuquerque generalization test under the same chronological split and metrics. Compared to the government-orientation baseline, VOTE-RAP improves rejected-class F1 (0.703 vs. 0.500) and AUROC (0.908 vs. 0.739 using the coarse probability mapping described in Section~\ref{sec:simple_baselines}). 

The Albuquerque-style model achieves AUROC of 0.500 and rejected-class F1 of 0.000 when tested on the full proposition space after being trained on the roll-call subset, \emph{performing identically to the majority-class baseline}. This demonstrates that approaches requiring roll-call data suffer from severe distribution shift: the roll-call subset has a 53\% pass rate, while the test set has an 86\% pass rate. When legislature-level features from the roll-call domain are applied to the broader proposition space, they provide no discriminative signal whatsoever. The model effectively defaults to predicting the majority class (approved), showing that patterns learned from the non-representative roll-call subset cannot transfer to the broader population. Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_auroc} and Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_f1_rejected} summarize these results.

\subsection{Minority Class Performance}
Rejected propositions form the minority class, making their detection sensitive to class imbalance and to the decision threshold. To prioritize rejected outcomes, we report rejected-class F1 at a threshold selected to maximize rejected-class F1 for the evaluated split. This post-hoc thresholding is used in our repository scripts to emphasize the minority class; a fully leakage-safe alternative would choose the threshold using only training-time validation predictions and then apply it once to the test set.

Under this protocol, VOTE-RAP achieves rejected-class F1 of 0.703, substantially improving upon the government-orientation baseline (0.500). At the selected threshold, precision and recall for the rejected class are 0.836 and 0.607, respectively, indicating that VOTE-RAP identifies rejections more effectively while maintaining high precision. Figure~\ref{fig:pr_curve} complements this analysis by comparing precision--recall curves across models on the test set; the legend reports average precision (AP) for the positive class, and the no-skill reference corresponds to the class prevalence in the test split (AP = 0.8632).

\subsection{Temporal Analysis}
To assess temporal robustness, we perform a year-by-year evaluation using a rolling window. For each test year \(y \in \{2007,\dots,2024\}\), we train an XGBoost model on the three preceding years (\(y-3\) to \(y-1\)) and evaluate it on year \(y\). Class imbalance is handled within each training window via a year-specific \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} computed from the window’s training labels, and we use the same rejected-class thresholding procedure described in Section~\ref{results}.
\par
Unless stated otherwise, the year-by-year results correspond to the \textbf{enhanced yearly setup}, i.e., the moving-window model that uses the full VOTE-RAP feature set (including \texttt{party\_\allowbreak popularity} and \texttt{historical\_\allowbreak approval\_\allowbreak rate} in addition to \texttt{gov\_\allowbreak orientation}, author popularity, and coalition-size indicators). Across the 18 test years, this enhanced setup attains mean accuracy of 0.811, mean rejected-class F1 of 0.624, and mean AUROC of 0.823, with noticeable variation over time. We observe lower performance in some years (e.g., 2015) and higher performance in others (e.g., 2019--2022), suggesting that predictive effectiveness varies with the legislative and political environment. \section{Final Remarks and Takeaways}
\label{discussion}

This section summarizes the main takeaways from our results, emphasizing the practical implications of full-coverage proposition-level outcome prediction, the interpretability of the proposed feature set, and the limitations observed when roll-call-dependent approaches are evaluated outside their native data regime.

\subsection{Interpretability and Transparency}
A key advantage of VOTE-RAP is its auditability: predictions are driven by a small set of explicitly defined features that encode institutional signals (\texttt{gov\_\allowbreak orientation}), coalition-size proxies (\texttt{num\_\allowbreak authors\_\allowbreak trunc} and \texttt{has\_\allowbreak more\_\allowbreak than\_\allowbreak 10\_\allowbreak authors}), and temporal evidence (\texttt{popularity}, \texttt{party\_\allowbreak popularity}, and \texttt{historical\_\allowbreak approval\_\allowbreak rate}). This structure enables direct inspection of why the model favors approval or rejection in a given case and supports transparent debugging when performance varies across time. Consistent with this design, the feature-importance analysis indicates that government orientation provides the strongest single signal, while coalition-size and temporal features contribute additional explanatory power beyond a single-feature baseline.
\par
Importantly, the ablation study in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that \texttt{gov\_\allowbreak orientation} alone does not achieve strong discrimination (AUROC 0.739), and that leakage-safe temporal evidence is responsible for most of the performance gain, mitigating concerns that the approach is merely reproducing an institutional recommendation.

The temporal features are also interpretable in substantive terms. Party popularity summarizes recent approval success for the proposition's party (or institutional author type), capturing short-run shifts in legislative support. HAR reflects proposition-specific memory by aggregating outcomes from prior voting sessions of the same proposition; when a proposition has historically been approved (or rejected) in earlier sessions, the model can leverage that information as an informative prior, while defaulting to a neutral value when no history exists.

\par
The generalization comparison against the Albuquerque-style approach reveals a fundamental limitation of roll-call-dependent methodologies. While \citet{Albuquerque2025Forecasting} achieve approximately 85\% accuracy when predicting individual deputy votes within the roll-call domain, this success does not transfer to the broader proposition space. The roll-call subset represents only 9\% of propositions and has a dramatically different class distribution (53\% pass rate vs. 80\% in the full dataset). When trained on this non-representative subset and tested on the full proposition space, the high-dimensional model achieves AUROC of 0.500, no better than random guessing. This demonstrates that approaches requiring roll-call data are fundamentally limited for practical legislative monitoring, where predictions are needed for all propositions, not just those with available roll-call votes. VOTE-RAP's full-coverage design, using features computable for any proposition, provides superior practical utility.


\subsection{Limitations}
The temporal evaluation shows that predictive performance is not uniform across years, suggesting sensitivity to changes in the legislative environment (e.g., coalition realignments or major political events). In addition, some predictors have incomplete coverage; in particular, government-orientation recommendations are missing or neutral in many sessions, limiting how often this strong institutional signal can be directly exploited. The \texttt{historical\_\allowbreak approval\_\allowbreak rate} feature is also only informative when propositions recur across multiple sessions; otherwise it defaults to a neutral prior. Finally, our rejected-class thresholding is selected to maximize rejected-class F1 for the evaluated split, which emphasizes minority-class detection but is not a fully leakage-safe deployment protocol unless the threshold is fixed using training-time validation predictions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{conclusion}

We presented VOTE-RAP, a feature-based approach for predicting proposition-level vote outcomes in the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies using leakage-safe temporal and structural signals, including \texttt{gov\_\allowbreak orientation}, coalition-size proxies, author popularity, party popularity (recency), and proposition-level historical approval rate (HAR). In our comprehensive benchmark under a chronological 80/20 split, VOTE-RAP achieves AUROC 0.908 and rejected-class F1 0.703, substantially outperforming the government-orientation baseline (AUROC 0.739, F1 0.500). 

Importantly, our generalization comparison demonstrates that high-dimensional approaches trained on limited roll-call data, such as the methodology proposed by \citet{Albuquerque2025Forecasting}, fail to generalize to the broader proposition space (AUROC 0.500 in the generalization test). This failure stems from the non-representative nature of the roll-call subset (only 9\% of propositions, with 53\% pass rate vs. 80\% in the full dataset), highlighting a fundamental limitation of roll-call-dependent approaches for practical legislative monitoring. VOTE-RAP's full-coverage design, using features computable for any proposition, provides superior practical utility while maintaining interpretability and auditability. Finally, a year-by-year moving-window analysis shows that performance varies across political periods, underscoring the importance of temporal evaluation in multiparty legislatures with shifting coalitions and evolving voting dynamics.

\par
From a practical standpoint, VOTE-RAP can support legislative monitoring as an early-warning system: by producing leakage-safe approval/rejection probabilities before the vote occurs, it can help teams prioritize which propositions to analyze more deeply (e.g., those predicted to be rejected despite recent approval momentum, or those with conflicting institutional and temporal signals), improving transparency and allocation of scarce analytic attention.

Future work can extend VOTE-RAP in three directions. First, richer temporal modeling (e.g., adaptive recency windows, temporal ensembling, or time-aware validation schemes) may capture dependencies beyond short fixed windows while maintaining leakage-safe evaluation. Second, incorporating additional context, including proposition text representations, committee routing, and other institutional metadata, may improve performance particularly when government orientation is unavailable. Third, multi-task setups that jointly model proposition outcomes and individual votes, as well as network-derived signals from voting graphs, may provide complementary structure and improve robustness during periods of political change.
